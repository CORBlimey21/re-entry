01 / THE PROBLEMENERGY & MOTION

Kinetic energy
in orbit

Kinetic energy is the energy an object has because it is moving.

A spacecraft in low Earth orbit travels at about 7,800 m/s. Gravity pulls it towards Earth, but its sideways speed keeps it moving around the planet. During re-entry, air resistance, called drag, slows the spacecraft and transfers much of its kinetic energy to the air.

Much of this energy is carried away by the hot air. Some reaches the spacecraft. Its thermal protection system limits how much heat passes through to the structure and cabin. [1][2]

02 / THE HEATINGSHOCKS & HEAT TRANSFER

Why re-entry
heats the air

Air is compressed and slowed across a shock wave ahead of the capsule.

Hypersonic means travelling at about five times the local speed of sound or faster. A curved shock wave, called a bow shock, forms just ahead of the capsule. As air crosses it, some of the energy of its forward motion becomes internal energy. Particles move more randomly, and energy can also excite molecules or break chemical bonds.

The hot gas heats the surface by convection, transferring energy from the moving gas, and by radiation, which carries energy as electromagnetic waves. Friction within the thin layer of air next to the surface also matters. This is the boundary layer. Surface friction alone cannot explain re-entry heating. [2][4]

How this model works & its limits +

Drag: FD = ½ρv²CDA. Here ρ is air density (kg/m³) and v is speed relative to the air (m/s). A is the area facing the airflow, set to 10 m². CD is the drag coefficient, which describes the effects of shape and flow. It is fixed at 1.2 here. These are example values, not measurements from a particular spacecraft. In reality, CD changes with the flow and the vehicle’s angle. [5]

Atmosphere: ρ(h) = ρ₀e−h/H, with ρ₀ = 1.217 kg/m³ and H = 8,500 m. This simple equation makes density fall with altitude. H is the scale height: every 8.5 km increase in height reduces the model’s density by a factor of e (about 2.72). A real atmosphere is more complicated, so this equation is not accurate throughout re-entry. [6][7]

Heating: q̇ ∝ √ρ v³ is an engineering approximation based on Sutton–Graves heating. It estimates how convective heat flux changes at the front of the capsule, where the airflow comes to a stop relative to it. The nose radius and gas are held fixed. Results are compared with the same model at 60 km and 7.8 km/s: 1 means the same heat flux, and 2 means twice as much. Because this is a ratio, the constant coefficient cancels. The model leaves out radiation, very thin-air effects (rarefied flow), changing gas chemistry, surface temperature and turbulence. It does not calculate a temperature or the total energy transferred during entry. [8]

TEMPERATURE / K

Temperature

Gas temperature is related to how much energy its particles have in their random motion. Very hot, thin gas may transfer less energy to a surface each second than cooler, denser gas.

HEAT FLUX / W m⁻²

Heat flux

Heat flux is the energy transferred each second to each square metre of surface, measured in W/m². Total heat exposure also depends on how long the heating lasts. It is the area under a heat-flux–time graph, measured in J/m². [9]

03 / WHEN AIR BECOMES PLASMAMATTER & COMMUNICATION

Plasma and
radio blackout

Some of the hot gas around the capsule can become electrically charged.

Collisions can give particles more energy. They can also break molecules into separate atoms. This is dissociation, and it uses energy to break chemical bonds. If an atom loses an electron, it becomes a charged ion: this is ionisation. A gas containing neutral particles, ions and free electrons is a partially ionised plasma.

Excited particles can release light as they return to lower energy states. The colour depends on which particles are present and their energy states, so colour alone does not tell us the temperature. How much plasma forms depends on the gas, its density and the time available for reactions. There is no single temperature or altitude at which all entry flows become plasma. [17][4][10]

04 / SURVIVING THE HEATTHERMAL PROTECTION SYSTEMS

How heat shields
protect a spacecraft

Thermal protection limits heat transfer to the spacecraft’s structure and cabin.

A wide, rounded heat shield holds the bow shock away from the surface. Under otherwise similar conditions, a larger nose radius reduces convective heating at the front of the capsule, where the airflow slows to a stop relative to it. [2]

An ablative heat shield gradually loses material as it heats. The outer material breaks down chemically and releases gases. The charred layer left behind slows conduction: heat passing through the shield. Radiation and escaping material also carry energy away. This process, called ablation, involves more than melting. [11]

PRINCIPLE 01 / HEAT CAPACITY
Q = mcΔT

Specific heat capacity c is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a material by 1 K. A higher value means more energy is needed for the same temperature rise. This equation assumes c stays roughly constant, with no change of state or chemical reaction. Ablation also involves energy used in decomposition and carried away by lost material. [9]

PRINCIPLE 02 / CONDUCTION
Q̇ = kAΔT / L

Thermal conductivity k describes how easily heat passes through a material. A lower k or a thicker layer L reduces the rate of heat transfer. This equation assumes a uniform, flat layer with steady heat flow in one direction. During re-entry, temperatures and material properties change with time. [12]

REUSABLE MATERIALS

Reusable
thermal protection

Reusable silica tiles conduct heat very slowly. They insulate the structure while their hot outer surfaces radiate energy away. They are intended to stay in place, so they must remain in good enough condition to be used again. [13]

Equation key: Q = transferred energy (J); m = mass (kg); c = specific heat capacity (J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹); ΔT = temperature difference (K); Q̇ = heat-transfer rate (W); k = thermal conductivity (W m⁻¹ K⁻¹); A = area (m²); L = thickness (m).

05 / ENGINEERING A SAFE DESCENTTHE ENTRY CORRIDOR

Choosing an
entry trajectory

The angle of entry affects how quickly the spacecraft slows down and how long it is heated.

A steep path takes the spacecraft into denser air quickly. This can cause stronger deceleration, meaning a faster decrease in speed, and a higher peak heat flux. A shallower path can spread the slowing over more time, but may increase total heat exposure. If it is too shallow, the spacecraft may leave the atmosphere again before slowing enough to complete the entry.

Engineers must limit both the highest heat flux and the total heat transferred over the entry. They also need to limit deceleration and reach the planned recovery area. The safe range of entry angles depends on the spacecraft and its starting speed and direction. [14]

CASE STUDY / NASA ORIONREAL MISSION EVIDENCE

Orion’s heat shield
after re-entry

11.12.2022ARTEMIS I / UNCREWED RETURN

Orion returned safely on 11 December 2022. Afterwards, inspectors found that parts of the charred surface of its Avcoat heat shield had broken away unexpectedly. NASA found that gas produced inside the material could not escape quickly enough. Pressure built up, causing cracks and pieces of char to break off.

The investigation linked the damage to Orion’s skip-entry trajectory. The capsule slowed during its first pass through the atmosphere, then climbed before descending again. Between these passes, lower heating rates allowed gas pressure to build inside the Avcoat. [11][15]

10.04.2026ARTEMIS II / CREWED RETURN

Artemis II splashed down on 10 April 2026. NASA’s October report said the heat shield worked as expected after changes to the entry path. Those changes reduced the time spent in the temperature range linked to the earlier problem. Inspectors found nine areas where char had broken away from the main heat-shield surface, compared with 103 after Artemis I. [16]

NASA published these findings on 15 October 2026. Assessment of heat-shield samples was still continuing at the time of that report.

THE QUESTION TO TEST

Does heating peak
at maximum speed?

Not necessarily. High in the atmosphere, speed can be high while density is very low. Deeper down, increasing density can raise heat flux even while the spacecraft is slowing.

The approximation used in the flow model gives q̇ ∝ √ρ v³ when the nose radius stays fixed. This means heat flux depends on both density and speed. Doubling density multiplies it by √2, while halving speed divides it by eight. Their combined changes determine the peak. This relationship describes convective heating; radiative heating follows different rules. [8]
